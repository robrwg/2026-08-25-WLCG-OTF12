The argument in one slide

Facility context and trust challenges

What everyone watches

Which model is best this month. The runtime. The demo that goes viral. This layer moves fast and is not ours to own.

What a facility actually owns

Context, identity, trust, packaging, and equitable access. Stable, hard, and exactly what WLCG sites already own.

The claim

An LLM becomes useful on your cluster the moment it knows your storage, your scheduler, your data, and your identity model — and stays safe only when those are real. That layer is a computing-infrastructure problem, and it ports across experiments. The rest is interchangeable.

Which leaves a facility two trust problems: AI acting on the facility (privileged actions a sysadmin would do) and AI acting through it (a physicist’s analysis, driven from their laptop). This talk is those two stories.

This is the spine of the whole talk; everything later is evidence or consequence. Deliver it as a thesis, then promise the structure: (1) a short existence proof from one WLCG site, (2) the physicist's view — laptop to facility through a credential-brokered gateway, (3) the sysadmin's view — how an agent earns trust for privileged actions and how we stay able to see what it knows, and (4) the WLCG question: what here, if anything, is worth coordinating. Map to the charge out loud once: (2) is data-management and interfaces, (3) is site operations, troubleshooting, and security. Keep this slide tight; it's the frame, not the evidence.

Grounding · fast vocabulary

Assumed vocabulary in this talk

Agent

A loop that uses an LLM to decide a step, call a tool, read the result, repeat.

MCP server

Model Context Protocol: a standard adapter exposing a service (Rucio, a notebook, a DB) as tools + live context any agent can call.

Skill

Static context loaded on demand: a reusable recipe (“how we do a fit here”).

Runtime

The app that gives the agent hands — runs commands, wires MCPs, enforces permissions (Claude Code, Codex, …). Also called a “harness.”

Bundle skills + MCPs + agents → a plugin; a discoverable collection of plugins → a marketplace.

Keep FAST — the OTF room is technical but not uniformly agent-literate. 60–90 s max. "Runtime" is the deliberate word choice; say once that the AI engineering community also says "harness" and move on. Timing: main-line but compressible.

Part 2 of 3 · AI acting through the facility

The physicist’s view: laptop → facility

The physicist of the near future hardly leaves the coding environment on their laptop. The facility’s job is a safe port to plug an agent into — who it is, what it may touch, and how every call is audited.

The MCP broker · pipelines from the laptop · identity.

Thesis to say: the physicist stays in their own coding environment; the facility's job is to be safely pluggable. One connector, like USB-C: the same socket regardless of which agent or model is on the other end. Covers the broker, intent-level pipelines, and identity.

The physicist’s view · the USB-C port of the facility

A credential-brokered MCP gateway

One standard socket in front of the facility’s services. The LLM client never holds raw credentials; the broker authenticates the user, mints short-lived per-service credentials, and audits every tool call.

The strategic boundary: identity, authZ, credential brokering, and audit in one place — the same boundary a site needs regardless of AI. Live at mcp.af.uchicago.edu: Giordon Stark’s af-mcp-platform.

The physicist’s view · identity is the bottleneck

Who is this client, and may it act as you?

Every MCP client — Claude, an internal tool, something a student wrote — wants to connect. Once agents are the ones connecting, this stops being a one-time setup step and becomes a continuous, scalable decision:

Who is it?a durable identity, not just a name
Do we trust it?policy, not vibes
How much authority?scoped, auditable

Identity is becoming the bottleneck, not an afterthought — and WLCG’s x509→OIDC transition is the backdrop. Today, most clients register the way DCR prescribes — the adopted, stateful mechanism. A newer draft spec, CIMD, proposes something stateless instead (a client’s identity as a URL the server fetches) — worth watching, not yet the default. This sits on top of the community’s federated-identity work — WLCG token profiles, the AARC blueprint, IAM — not instead of it.

SIMPLIFIED main line (moved DCR/CIMD jargon to the two backups) — for a mixed colloquium room, the memorable takeaway is "identity becomes the bottleneck," not "DCR vs CIMD." Say the three questions out loud as the research problem. IMPORTANT ACCURACY NOTE: don't imply CIMD is "the solution" — DCR is what's actually adopted today (an approved RFC, what OIDC-based MCP clients follow in practice); CIMD is still an IETF draft, not yet a real-world default, even though the MCP spec itself prefers it (SHOULD vs MAY). Frame CIMD as "a newer proposal worth watching," not "the standard." AARC/WLCG line matters for Groep/Campana in the room. This is a place to shape the standard, not just consume it — a facility/Tier-1 voice in how research infrastructure adopts client-identity models. Deep-dive · CIMD vs DCR

Client ID = a URL the server fetches

GET /authorize?client_id=https://app.example/oauth.json&…
        │  authn server fetches the JSON, validates, applies policy
        ▼
{ "client_id":"https://app.example/oauth.json",
  "client_name":"…","redirect_uris":[…] }   ← hosted by the client
  • Wins: no /register abuse surface; stable identity per app; domain-based allow/deny.
  • Costs: server makes outbound fetches → SSRF risk (block private IPs, HTTPS-only, size/timeout caps); localhost ambiguity; domain ≠ trust.

Concretely in FastMCP: CIMDTrustPolicy(trusted_domains=["claude.ai","cursor.com"], auto_approve_trusted=True) — the facility decides which domains skip consent.

Auth today reuses grid creds (x509) or Keycloak OIDC — OAuth bridge docs.
BACKUP. The client_id IS the HTTPS URL to a metadata doc the client hosts; the authn server fetches it on demand, validates client_id == source URL and the redirect_uris, then applies its own policy. Read-only, stateless, fetch-on-demand. Spec status: adopted into MCP 2025-11-25 via SEP-991, from an IETF draft promoted to WG status Oct 2025 (origins in IndieAuth; in production at Bluesky); MCP Python SDK added minimal client-side support in v1.23.0, server-side still maturing. Stance: CIMD = SHOULD, DCR = MAY, pre-registration still allowed. Software statements (RFC 7591 §2.3) / OS attestation are the next layer if asked. The second backup (down-arrow) has a real trace, the actual Claude client_id URLs, and the full spec list — pull that up only if someone like Groep or Campana pushes on mechanics.

Part 3 of 3 · AI acting on the facility

The sysadmin’s view: earning trust for privileged actions

The actions that matter are the ones an admin does with privileges. An agent gets them only the way a new human operator would: incrementally, observed, and revocable — and the admins must always be able to see what it did and what it has learned.

The trust ladder · governance in git · three levels · memory · enforcement.

Thesis to say: this is the part of the talk for everyone who runs a site. Nothing here grants trust up front; autonomy is earned action-type by action-type, recorded in reviewable artifacts, and revocable. The next five slides are the mechanics: ladder, git governance, levels, memory, enforcement.

The sysadmin’s view · earned autonomy

A trust ladder, with promotion gates

Observe

read-only diagnostics (the HTCondor monitor)

Suggest

drafts a fix; a human approves

Submit job

acts, scoped and reversible

Restart service

bounces a stuck transformer / worker

Operate production

remediates Ceph, deploys services

How does an agent earn the next rung? Evaluation as the promotion gate: curated fault scenarios in a staging environment, scored, regression-tested when the model changes. Autonomy is measured, not assumed — the same way society learned to trust autonomous vehicles: evidence first, hands off the wheel later, and never everywhere at once.

The Waymo model, named out loud: driver present, then hands off the wheel on mapped streets, then no driver — each step gated by accumulated evidence, bounded by geography (for us: bounded by blast radius). Concrete ops verbs on the cards (Observe -> Suggest -> Submit job -> Restart service -> Operate production). The eval-as-promotion-gate idea comes from the IRIS-HEP/OSG-LHC concepts doc (trust ladder + facility-ops eval harness: injected faults, scored outcomes, regression on model change). This is the governance answer to "what may an agent do?" — quantified reliability a site operations team can defend to its own management.

The sysadmin’s view · where the rules live

Governance lives in a git repository

Principles

Immutable commitments: auditability, minimal privilege, human-in-the-loop until trust is earned, reproducibility.

Policies

Scoped YAML rules derived from them: “storage access read-only by default,” “no outbound web unless listed,” “wait for human ack after restart.”

Runbooks — the atomic unit of trust

A human-reviewed, PR-gated spec per capability: what it does, what it may do autonomously, what requires a human.

governance/
├── principles/
│   ├── auditability.md
│   └── minimal-privilege.md
├── policies/
│   ├── condor/query-rate.yaml
│   └── network/egress.yaml
└── runbooks/
    ├── htcondor/queue-health.md
    └── kubernetes/cluster-health.md

AI drafts from cluster ground truth → the domain expert reviews and corrects → merged = blessed → runbook + agent config = a deployed capability. The PR history is the audit record.

The tools sysadmins already trust — git, code review, PRs — become the trust machinery for agents. Nothing exotic: a principle is a value, a policy is that value made specific to one service, a runbook is the contract for one capability. The runbook is deliberately the atomic unit of trust: an action type is either listed as autonomous or it requires a human, and changing that list is itself a PR. Say out loud: "if you can review a colleague's change, you can review an agent's charter." This and the next two slides are the concepts behind our UChicago implementation; the framework names are deliberately left off — the concepts are what port.

The sysadmin’s view · blast radius, not bureaucracy

Three governance levels, one progression

Level 1 · Assisted

AI drafts runbooks and diagnoses; humans execute everything. No autonomous action. Blast radius: a document.

Level 2 · User-scope agents

Agents act for a physicist, with that user’s credentials only — no admin access, ever. Blast radius: one user’s quota.

Level 3 · Privileged automation

Scheduled agents with scoped service accounts, read-only defaults, explicit write allowlists, PR-gated changes. Blast radius: bounded by the account.

Dev sandboxprove the pattern
Staging clusterprove it under load
Productiononly after both

Every capability climbs both stairs independently: governance level (what it may do) and environment (where it may do it). Nothing skips a step — the framing is blast radius, not compliance.

Level 1 is where every site can start tomorrow: AI-assisted runbook writing, humans at the keyboard. Level 2 is the physicist story from Part 2 — the agent holds the user's own credentials and nothing more, so the worst case is what that user could already do. Level 3 is the interesting one for this room: scheduled, privileged automation, made tolerable by scoped service accounts, read-only defaults, explicit write allowlists, and PR-gated promotion. The environment progression is mandatory at our site: a dev sandbox cluster, then a staging cluster, then the AF — patterns are proven twice before they touch production. Say: "the question we ask is never 'is it smart enough' but 'what is the blast radius if it is wrong.'"

The sysadmin’s view · watching the watcher

Memory: how admins see what the agent knows

Normative source

Runbooks, policies, agent configs. Human-written, PR-gated. What the agent may do.

Runtime state record

Auto-pushed to git: accumulated knowledge + daily session logs. Agent-written, human-reviewed. What it has learned.

DR archive

Daily snapshots of agent state and conversations. Restart = restore + wait for human ack.

Session memory

Live in-process state. Ephemeral by design — lost on crash, and that’s fine.

Runbook bootstraps the agent
Agent accumulates knowledge
Team reviews weekly
Durable insight → runbook, via PR

And when an action type moves from human-approval to autonomous, that is itself a PR to the agent’s config — a trust escalation log. You record the moment you stopped watching.

This is the answer to "how would I even know what it's doing?" — the question every experienced admin asks first. Two distinctions to say out loud: runbooks are read-only and procedural (what it CAN do); memory is read-write and experiential (what it HAS learned) — a window into the running agent's mind, in git, diffable, reviewable at the weekly ops meeting like any other change. The promotion loop is the payoff: the agent's operational experience flows back into the human-owned runbook only through review. And the trust escalation log is the single most quotable governance idea we have: autonomy changes are themselves version-controlled — you can point to the commit where you stopped watching, who approved it, and on what evidence.

The sysadmin’s view · enforcement

Enforcement is dual — and sandboxing is real work

Two mechanisms, both required: policy awareness inside the agent’s context (it reasons about the rule before acting) and technical enforcement outside it (RBAC, scoped service accounts, network isolation, allowlists). Never one without the other:

Podsk8s default: caps, no host access
Sandboxoff k8s: bubblewrap, sydbox
PolicyeBPF-class, fine-grained
Privilegeearned, human-gated

The honest gap

Today a runtime can advise (managed CLAUDE.md, allow-lists) but can’t reliably hard-deny a command at the user level. Real blocking needs a privileged policy engine on the node, or a sandbox — as a fellow facility sysadmin put it, an agent just lets users 100× the bad behaviour we’ve had for decades.

Deep-dive · how do you actually enforce it?

Pods, cgroups, and eBPF

Coarse: containers / cgroups

Resource isolation (CPU/mem/io), bind-mounts to hide paths, seccomp to drop syscalls. Blunt but simple.

Fine: eBPF

Sandboxed kernel programs attached to a hook point — a syscall, a kernel tracepoint, a kprobe/uprobe almost anywhere. Tetragon, Falco, bpflock.

cgroups/pods and eBPF are orthogonal: resource isolation vs fine-grained syscall/file policy. An agent may need both — a pod for blast-radius, a policy engine for “don’t find / the shared filesystem.”

eBPF: ebpf.io/what-is-ebpf · agent-focused eBPF observability: agentsight.
BACKUP for the sysadmin/security crowd (Campana likely probes here). Diagram redrawn from ebpf.io's own hook-point explanation: "eBPF programs are event-driven and run when the kernel or an application passes a certain hook point. Pre-defined hooks include system calls, function entry/exit, kernel tracepoints, network events; if none exists, attach a kprobe/uprobe almost anywhere." From Greg Thain (HTCondor): eBPF was historically read-only; BPF-LSM now allows some mutation of kernel state/syscall results (already replaced k8s networking-proxy sidecars with in-kernel control). Lincoln Bryant: cgroups = resource isolation, eBPF = attach to kernel funcs/data. Greg's HTC'24 eBPF talk is the reference. Tools: Tetragon, Falco, bpflock; sydbox (gitlab.exherbo.org/sydbox/sydbox); bubblewrap. NEW: agentsight (eunomia-bpf) is a perf/top/strace-like tool FOR AI AGENTS specifically — eBPF probes watch an agent's process tree, file ops, network destinations, and (via TLS tracing at the syscall boundary) even the plaintext LLM request/response, all without modifying the agent. That's the connective tissue between "eBPF as a concept" and "eBPF as how you'd actually observe what an agent did on this facility" — a real answer to "can you audit what the agent touched?" research.md §sandboxing has more.

Agenda · a security reality

Agents don’t get the open web

  • Facilities (NERSC, UChicago, SLAC, BNL, AmSC, NRP…) are blocking web access for agents to defend against prompt injection from untrusted pages.
  • So a skill can’t just say “go read the docs.” It must encode just enough guidance — and delegate “what’s the latest?” to a curated MCP, not the open internet.

Stale-prone

use /path/2024-egamma-recs.config

a hardcoded path, wrong within a season.

Durable

“ask the chATLAS MCP for the latest recommendation path, then configure.”

the MCP owns “what’s current.”

The design rule: minimal durable guidance + a trusted live source. What an agent may reach is a facility decision, not a user convenience.

The landscape question · options, not a proposal

What is site-specific, and what could be common?

The governance model and interface patterns port; the policies, playbooks, and trust surfaces stay local. In between sits a menu of candidates — each could become a WLCG activity, an HSF-style community project, or stay experiment- and site-specific:

Standardize the port

a conformant facility-MCP interface profile — the way WLCG already standardizes site interfaces

Authorize the clients

common conventions for agentic clients on WLCG tokens (client identity, token exchange, audit)

Share the knowledge

a commons of runbooks and skills — single-source, reviewed, not a dozen forks

Operate together

shared evaluation scenarios for ops agents; comparable trust ladders across sites

What likely doesn’t need coordination: model choice, runtime choice, per-site policy content. The question for this forum is which of the four — if any — benefit from doing it together.

AARC · EOSC · ESCAPE · IRIS-HEP/OSG-LHC concepts doc.

Agenda · where context lives

One commons, not a dozen forks

  • Skills are the durable context layer — but who hosts them, who maintains them, and how do we stop every experiment forking the same skill?
  • A live debate in ATLAS right now: a single-source marketplace (reference skills by URL, fix upstream) vs duplicated copies; docs kept next to the source code; official, e-group-governed repos over personal ones. Still open: how to link, not copy (usatlas/marketplace#60).
  • As the library grows, semantic confusability bites (overlapping descriptions degrade routing) → skill-design guides + an eval/lint harness to keep skills orthogonal.
  • A third option worth watching: package skills like software. prefix.dev’s skill-forge distributes agent skills as versioned conda/pixi packages — lockfile discipline applied to context instead of code.

US ATLAS already operates a marketplace others are converging toward (following agentskills.io practice). The open question for WLCG: what is the cross-experiment, cross-region contribution to that commons? ESCAPE is one model for how such a collaboration forms and persists. On the US side, an AI proto-collaboration (umbrella over IRIS-HEP and other funded projects + research groups, open to international collaborators) updated at DPF, July 2026.

Agenda · equitable access to inference

Who gets to use the models — and who pays?

  • If agentic analysis becomes normal, access to inference becomes a fairness issue: a credit-card-per-student model entrenches inequality between groups and countries.
  • A facility can host open-weight models on its own GPUs (NRP-style) for equitable, cost-controlled baseline access…
  • …and host the foundational models experiments depend on, so reconstruction/inference services aren’t hostage to a single vendor or budget line.
  • Bridge identity, don’t fork it: exchange a scientist’s WLCG/SciTokens credential for a scoped token an inference backend accepts (OAuth 2.0 Token Exchange, RFC 8693) — MCP-aware, so it scopes which models and which tools.

This is squarely a computing-infrastructure responsibility: GPUs, model hosting, and a fair-share policy are facility decisions, not personal ones. The cost is already real — a full agentic analysis runs to ~1M+ tokens (illustrative), tens to hundreds of dollars per run at frontier rates (numbers below).

Agents already perform full HEP analyses end-to-end — Moreno et al., arXiv:2603.20179 — and that run kept stalling on usage rate-limits: access is already the binding constraint.

Per analysis: tens to hundreds of dollars

$100–200+

GPU time only

Self-hosting open weights on facility GPUs collapses the per-run cost to ~GPU time — the equity lever. A credit-card-per-student model does the opposite, between groups and between countries.

Not engineering tickets — research questions

What we don’t know yet

How should facilities expose context?

One MCP per service or a gateway delegating identity? DCR or CIMD? Where does a site’s audit live?

How do we share context between facilities?

Shipped with the facility, or a shared commons? Single-source vs forks; eval/lint for orthogonality; who keeps it true?

How do we evaluate — and secure — an autonomous operator?

Human gates, k8s vs bubblewrap/eBPF isolation, credential brokering so agents never hold raw secrets, a trust ladder with an eval-harness promotion gate.

What makes an environment truly portable?

pixi-native batch as the default? Supply-chain policy for conda-forge?

Who pays for inference, and is that fair?

Hosted frontier vs self-hosted open weights on facility GPUs; equity as policy.

What becomes standardized, across sciences?

A commons spanning AFs, LHC experiments, and astroparticle (KM3NeT, Virgo, XENON) via ESCAPE — where’s the facility/experiment line?

Our bet: the model and runtime are the easy part. Context, identity, trust, and equity are the facility’s homework.
